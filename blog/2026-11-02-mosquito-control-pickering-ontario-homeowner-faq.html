If you live in Pickering, you already know the feeling of trying to enjoy a summer evening on the patio only to end up swatting mosquitoes within minutes. Between the waterfront along Lake Ontario, the ravines that cut through many neighbourhoods, and the ponds in newer subdivisions, Pickering has no shortage of spots for mosquitoes to breed. This FAQ-style guide answers the questions we hear most often from homeowners across Durham Region, with a focus on what actually helps in a Pickering backyard.

Why Does Pickering Seem to Have So Many Mosquitoes?

Pickering's geography is part of the problem. The city sits along the Lake Ontario shoreline and includes several creek systems and wooded ravines, all of which hold moisture well into summer. Add in backyard ponds, decorative water features, and the usual suburban clutter of rain barrels, kiddie pools, and clogged gutters, and you've got a lot of potential nurseries for mosquito larvae. Mosquitoes don't need much water to breed either; a bottle cap's worth left undisturbed for a week can be enough.

Homeowners in nearby Ajax and Whitby deal with a similar setup, since much of Durham Region shares the same lakefront and ravine landscape. If you've noticed mosquitoes seem worse some years than others, it usually comes down to rainfall and temperature patterns more than anything unique to one street or subdivision.

What Can Homeowners Do Before Calling a Professional?

Before you reach for a spray can or call in help, it's worth doing a quick walk around your property. Most mosquito problems start closer to home than people expect.

  • Empty or cover anything that collects rainwater, including buckets, wheelbarrows, plant saucers, and recycling bins
  • Clean out eavestroughs so water doesn't pool and sit for days at a time
  • Check for low spots in the lawn or driveway where puddles tend to linger after rain
  • Change the water in birdbaths and pet bowls every few days
  • Keep grass and shrubs trimmed, since mosquitoes rest in shaded, overgrown vegetation during the day

These steps won't eliminate mosquitoes entirely, especially if you're near a ravine or creek where neighbouring properties and public land are also breeding grounds, but they do reduce the number that are actually coming from your own yard.

When Does Mosquito Season Really Start and End in Durham Region?

In most years, mosquito activity in Pickering and the surrounding Durham Region starts to pick up in May as temperatures warm and spring rain fills low-lying areas. Activity usually peaks from June through August, then tapers off through September as nights cool down. A wet summer, or a sudden heat wave followed by heavy rain, can trigger a noticeable second wave of mosquitoes even in late August. This is why a lot of homeowners find that a single spray early in the season isn't enough on its own, and why seasonal programs that return every few weeks tend to hold up better through the whole stretch.

What Makes a Seasonal Program Different From a One-Time Spray

A single treatment can knock down the mosquito population you've got right now, but new mosquitoes hatch on a cycle of roughly one to two weeks depending on temperature. That means a one-time spray often wears off right around the time a fresh batch is becoming active. A seasonal program is built around that cycle, with visits scheduled every few weeks through the warm months to keep breaking the cycle before it restarts. Mosquito Man Plus offers a return-visit guarantee on seasonal programs, so if mosquitoes are still a problem between scheduled visits, we come back out.

This approach tends to make the biggest difference for properties backing onto ravines, ponds, or wooded lots, which describes a lot of homes in Pickering as well as in Ajax and Clarington. Our mosquito control services are built around this kind of ongoing protection rather than a single quick fix.

Does Location Within Pickering Affect Treatment Needs?

Yes, to some degree. Homes closer to the waterfront trails, Frenchman's Bay, or one of Pickering's many ravines tend to see more mosquito pressure than homes in drier, more open subdivisions further from water. The same pattern shows up elsewhere in Durham Region. A property in Brooklin near wooded lots will often have different mosquito pressure than one in a newer part of Courtice with less mature landscaping. When we assess a property, we look at these specific features, shade cover, standing water, vegetation density, rather than applying a one-size-fits-all plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does mosquito control cost for a typical Pickering backyard?

Cost depends on lot size, how much vegetation and standing water is on the property, and whether you choose a one-time treatment or a seasonal program. A property assessment is the best way to get an accurate quote for your specific yard.

How long does a mosquito treatment last?

A single treatment typically provides a few weeks of noticeable reduction, which is why seasonal programs with repeat visits every few weeks tend to work better over a full summer in Durham Region.

Will treating my yard affect bees or other pollinators?

Products are applied to resting and breeding areas like shaded vegetation and standing water rather than broadly across flowering plants, and any specific pollinator gardens can be flagged ahead of time so the technician can adjust the approach.

My house backs onto a ravine in Pickering, will treatment still help?

Yes, though properties near ravines or creeks usually see more mosquito pressure from surrounding natural habitat, so a seasonal program is generally more effective than a single treatment in these spots.

Do I need to do anything before a technician arrives?

It helps to clear or empty any standing water you can find, such as buckets, saucers, or clogged gutters, since this reduces breeding sites the technician doesn't have direct access to treat.
