Tick Control Durham: Myths vs. Facts for Homeowners

TL;DR

Ticks are increasingly common across Durham Region, and a lot of what homeowners believe about them is outdated or simply wrong. This post breaks down common tick myths versus the facts, and explains what actually reduces tick risk in yards across Oshawa, Whitby, Ajax, Pickering, and beyond. Practical yard habits combined with professional tick treatments give the best results.

Key Takeaways

  • Ticks are active any time temperatures rise above freezing, not just in summer
  • Ticks don't jump or fly - they wait on tall grass and brush for a host to brush past
  • Backyard tick risk is closely tied to yard edges, woodpiles, and leaf litter, especially near wooded areas common in Durham Region
  • Pets can carry ticks into the yard even if the pet never goes near a wooded area
  • Professional tick treatments target the actual habitat ticks use, which is more effective than relying on repellent alone

If you've spent any time outdoors in Durham Region over the past few summers, you've probably noticed more talk about ticks than there used to be. Homeowners in Oshawa, Whitby, and Ajax are asking pest control companies about tick risk more than ever, and along with that curiosity comes a fair amount of misinformation. Some of it is harmless. Some of it leads people to skip precautions that actually matter. Let's sort out what's true and what's not.

Myth: Ticks Are Only a Problem in Deep Woods

A lot of people assume ticks are strictly a hiking-trail problem, something you only need to worry about if you're deep in a conservation area. That's not accurate. Ticks live wherever there's tall grass, leaf litter, brush piles, or the edge of a wooded lot, and plenty of properties across Durham Region back onto exactly that kind of habitat. A yard in Clarington or Courtice that borders a treeline can have just as much tick activity as a trail in a provincial park. The key factor isn't distance from "the wilderness," it's the type of ground cover in your immediate yard.

Fact: Yard Edges Are the Real Risk Zone

Ticks don't like open, mowed lawn. They prefer the transition zones, the unmowed strip along a fence, an overgrown garden bed, a stack of firewood, or a shaded area where leaves pile up in fall and never really dry out. If your property has any of these features, that's where a tick inspection or treatment should focus, not the middle of the lawn.

Myth: Ticks Jump or Fly Onto People

This is one of the most common misunderstandings we hear. Ticks can't jump, and they can't fly. They practice something called "questing," where they climb to the tip of a blade of grass or a low branch and wait with their front legs extended, ready to grab onto anything that brushes past. That means the moment of exposure usually happens when someone (or a pet) walks through tall grass or brush, not from a tick dropping out of a tree.

Fact: Brushing Against Vegetation Is the Main Transfer Point

Because of how questing works, keeping grass mowed short and clearing brush along walking paths meaningfully reduces contact. This is especially relevant for families in Ajax and Pickering with kids or dogs who cut through wooded edges to reach a park or trail. A simple habit like staying on cleared paths and doing a quick tick check afterward goes a long way.

Myth: Cold Canadian Winters Kill Off All the Ticks

It would be convenient if a hard frost wiped the slate clean every year, but that's not how it works. Ticks can survive winter in leaf litter, under snow cover, or on hosts like deer and rodents. They also become active again any time temperatures climb above roughly 4°C, which in Durham Region can happen in March or even during a mild stretch in February. That means "tick season" is really any time the ground isn't frozen solid, not just June through August.

Fact: Early Spring Checks Matter More Than Most People Think

Because ticks can be active so early in the year, waiting until summer to think about yard treatment often means missing the first wave of activity. Homeowners who get ahead of the season, particularly those with properties near wooded lots in Bowmanville, Newcastle, or Uxbridge, tend to have fewer tick encounters overall.

Myth: If My Pet Doesn't Go in the Woods, We're Fine

Pets pick up ticks in surprisingly ordinary places, along a backyard fence line, in tall grass at the edge of a schoolyard, or during a walk through a park that looks perfectly manicured near the entrance but has overgrown sections further in. A tick doesn't need a deep forest to find a host. It just needs unmowed vegetation and a passing animal. Dogs in particular are efficient at bringing ticks from outside spaces directly into the house.

Fact: Yard Treatment Reduces the Ticks That Reach Pets and People in the First Place

Treating the yard itself, rather than relying only on pet collars or spot-on treatments, addresses the source of the problem. It's a layered approach: personal protection matters, but so does reducing the tick population in the spaces where people and pets actually spend time.

Myth: One Backyard Spray Solves the Whole Problem

Tick control isn't a single event. Because ticks have a multi-stage life cycle and can be reintroduced by wildlife like deer, rodents, and even birds, ongoing management works better than a one-time treatment. This is true whether you're in a more rural setting like Port Perry or Scugog, or a suburban neighbourhood in Whitby with a wooded ravine nearby.

What Actually Helps: Practical Steps for Durham Region Yards

Setting the myths aside, here's what genuinely makes a difference for homeowners across the GTA and Durham Region:

  • Keep grass mowed short, especially along fence lines and garden edges
  • Clear leaf litter and brush piles each fall, since they act as tick habitat over winter
  • Create a buffer of wood chips or gravel between lawn and wooded areas
  • Check pets and family members after time spent near tall grass or trails
  • Consider a professional tick treatment for properties bordering wooded lots or ravines

Every property is a little different. A yard in North York with a small shaded corner has a different risk profile than a rural property near Brock or Scugog with several acres of tree cover. That's why a site-specific look at your yard tends to be more useful than generic advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

When does tick season actually start in Durham Region?

Ticks can become active any time temperatures rise above about 4°C, which sometimes happens as early as March. It's safer to start yard precautions in early spring rather than waiting until summer.

Can ticks live in a regular suburban backyard, or only near forests?

Ticks can live in any yard with tall grass, leaf litter, or brush, including suburban properties in Oshawa, Whitby, or Ajax that simply border a treeline or ravine. You don't need to live next to a large forest for ticks to be a concern.

Do ticks jump onto people from trees?

No, ticks can't jump or fly. They wait on grass or low brush and grab onto a person or animal that brushes past, which is why staying on cleared paths helps reduce exposure.

Will one tick treatment for my yard take care of the problem for the whole year?

A single treatment can help, but ticks are often reintroduced by wildlife like deer and rodents, so ongoing seasonal treatment tends to give more consistent results. Return visits allow adjustments as conditions change through the season.

My dog doesn't go into the woods, so why does it keep getting ticks?

Ticks don't need deep woods to survive, they can live in tall grass along a fence line, at the edge of a park, or in an overgrown garden bed. Pets often pick up ticks in these ordinary, everyday spaces rather than in remote areas.
