Why Mosquitoes Are Persistent Around Courtice Ontario

Courtice sits close to several waterways, including Black Creek and tributaries that feed into Lake Ontario, along with low-lying residential areas that tend to hold moisture after rain. Mosquitoes only need a small amount of standing water, sometimes as little as a bottle cap's worth, to lay eggs and complete their life cycle. Combine that with mature trees and shaded yards, which keep humidity high and temperatures cooler during the day, and you get conditions mosquitoes find hard to resist.

This isn't unique to Courtice. Much of Durham Region shares similar geography, which is part of why neighbouring communities like Clarington and Bowmanville deal with the same seasonal patterns. If you want a broader picture of how these conditions play out across the region, our complete Durham Region mosquito control guide walks through the full season timeline in more detail.

When Mosquito Season Typically Peaks in Durham Region

In most years, mosquito activity in this part of Ontario ramps up in May as temperatures warm and lingering spring moisture provides breeding sites. Activity usually stays high through June and July, especially after rainy stretches, and tapers off gradually through September. Some years see a second smaller spike in late summer if there's a run of humid, wet weather.

Homeowners in Courtice often notice that certain parts of the yard are worse than others. Shaded corners, areas near fences or sheds, and spots close to drainage ditches tend to hold more mosquitoes than open, sunny lawns. Paying attention to these patterns on your own property is a useful first step before deciding whether you need more than basic prevention.

Practical Steps Courtice Homeowners Can Take Now

Before considering professional treatment, there are a handful of habits that make a real difference. None of these require special equipment, just a bit of regular attention around the property.

  • Empty and scrub birdbaths, plant saucers, and pet water bowls at least once a week
  • Clear leaves and debris from eavestroughs so water doesn't pool
  • Check tarps, wheelbarrows, and kids' toys for collected rainwater after a storm
  • Keep grass and shrubs trimmed to reduce shaded resting spots for adult mosquitoes
  • Store firewood and compost piles away from the house and off the ground where possible

These steps won't eliminate mosquitoes entirely, especially if your property backs onto a creek or wooded area, but they do reduce the number of breeding sites close to the house. For many homeowners in Courtice and nearby Bowmanville, this kind of routine maintenance combined with occasional treatment is enough to make outdoor evenings tolerable again.

When Yard Maintenance Isn't Enough

Some properties need more than good habits. If your yard backs onto wetlands, has mature tree cover, or simply gets more mosquito pressure than the typical lot, DIY sprays and citronella candles tend to offer short-lived relief at best. This is usually where a scheduled treatment approach makes sense, targeting resting areas in vegetation as well as potential breeding sites that aren't always obvious from a quick walk around the yard.

What a Seasonal Mosquito Treatment Involves

A typical seasonal program starts with an assessment of the property to identify likely breeding and resting sites, things like dense shrubs, low-lying wet spots, and areas with heavy shade. Treatment is then applied using EPA and PMRA-registered products, focused on the vegetation and surfaces where adult mosquitoes rest during the day. Because mosquitoes continue to emerge from nearby water sources throughout the season, follow-up visits are typically scheduled every few weeks rather than as a single application.

This is also where a return-visit guarantee on seasonal programs matters. Weather, rainfall, and nearby untreated properties can all affect how long a treatment lasts, so having scheduled returns built into the plan helps keep coverage consistent rather than relying on a single visit to last the whole summer.
